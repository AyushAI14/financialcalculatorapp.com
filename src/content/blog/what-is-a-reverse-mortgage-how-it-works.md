"What is a reverse mortgage, and how does it actually work?"

For millions of older homeowners sitting on substantial home equity but feeling squeezed by inflation and fixed retirement incomes, the promise of a reverse mortgage sounds almost too good to be true: **convert your home equity into tax-free cash with zero monthly mortgage payments.**

Commercials promote them as easy retirement supplements, while critics warn they can lead to losing the family home.

To make an informed decision for yourself or an aging parent, you need to understand the real financial mechanics. How do traditional mortgages differ from reverse mortgages? How are monthly payments and loan balances calculated? And what are the critical traps that can trigger foreclosure?

Here is the complete guide and mathematical breakdown.

---

## 1. What is a Reverse Mortgage?

A **reverse mortgage** is a specialized loan that allows homeowners aged **62 and older** to borrow against the accumulated equity in their primary residence without making monthly mortgage payments to a bank.

Instead of making payments to a lender every month—as you do with a traditional "forward" mortgage—**the lender makes payments to you** (or provides a lump sum or credit line).

You retain title and ownership of your home. You do not have to repay the borrowed balance until you sell the home, move out permanently (such as into assisted living for more than 12 consecutive months), or pass away.

### The Most Common Type: HECM
Over 90% of all reverse mortgages in the United States are **Home Equity Conversion Mortgages (HECMs)**, which are insured by the Federal Housing Administration (FHA) through the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD).

Because HECMs are government-backed, they include a mandatory **non-recourse clause**: neither you nor your heirs will ever owe more than the home’s fair market value when the loan is repaid, even if the loan balance grows to exceed the value of the house.

## 2. Forward Mortgage vs. Reverse Mortgage: The Core Differences

To understand how a reverse mortgage works, it helps to compare it directly to a standard forward mortgage:

| Feature | Traditional (Forward) Mortgage | Reverse Mortgage (HECM) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cash Flow Direction** | You pay the lender every month | The lender pays you (or eliminates your existing payment) |
| **Loan Balance Over Time** | **Decreases** monthly through amortization | **Increases** monthly through negative amortization |
| **Home Equity Over Time** | **Increases** over time | **Decreases** over time |
| **Minimum Age Requirement** | 18+ years old | 62+ years old (HUD/FHA rule) |
| **Monthly Payment Obligation** | Mandatory principal + interest payments | **$0 monthly mortgage payment** required |
| **Ongoing Tax & Insurance Duty** | Required | **Required** (failure risks foreclosure) |
| **Repayment Timing** | Fixed schedule (e.g., 15 or 30 years) | Due when you move, sell, or pass away |
| **Tax Status of Proceeds** | N/A (borrowed money to buy property) | **Tax-free** (considered loan advances, not income) |

## 3. How to Calculate Mortgage Payments: Forward vs. Reverse

One of the most frequent questions from homeowners is: *how are mortgage payments actually calculated?* The mathematical mechanics depend entirely on whether the loan is a forward or reverse mortgage.

### A. How Standard Forward Mortgage Payments are Calculated
For a traditional fixed-rate mortgage, the monthly Principal & Interest ($M$) payment is calculated using the standard **annuity amortization formula**:

$$M = P \cdot \frac{r(1 + r)^n}{(1 + r)^n - 1}$$

Where:
* $P$ = Loan principal (purchase price minus down payment)
* $r$ = Monthly interest rate ($\text{annual rate} \div 12$)
* $n$ = Total number of monthly payments ($\text{term in years} \times 12$)

#### Practical Example:
Suppose you borrow **$320,000** on a 30-year fixed loan at **6.5% interest**:
* $r = 0.065 / 12 = 0.0054167$
* $n = 30 \times 12 = 360$
* Monthly P&I ($M$) = **$2,022.62**

To find your true monthly housing outlay, you must add **PITI**:
$$\text{Total Monthly Housing Payment} = M + \text{Property Taxes} + \text{Hazard Insurance} + \text{HOA} + \text{PMI}$$

If property taxes are $400/month and insurance is $125/month, your total payment is **$2,547.62/month**.

---

### B. How Reverse Mortgage Payouts and Balances are Calculated
A reverse mortgage operates in reverse through **negative amortization**. Instead of payments shrinking your debt, interest and fees are added to the loan balance each month.

#### Step 1: Calculating the Principal Limit (How Much You Can Borrow)
You cannot borrow 100% of your home equity. HUD uses a strict formula to determine your **Principal Limit**:

$$\text{Principal Limit} = \text{Maximum Claim Amount (MCA)} \times \text{Principal Limit Factor (PLF)}$$

1. **Maximum Claim Amount (MCA):** The lesser of your home's appraised market value or the federal FHA maximum lending ceiling.
2. **Principal Limit Factor (PLF):** A percentage dictated by HUD tables based on two variables:
   * **Age of the youngest borrower** (older borrowers receive higher percentages).
   * **Current interest rate** (lower interest rates unlock higher borrowing limits).

| Age of Youngest Borrower | Approx. PLF (at 6.5% Expected Rate) | Borrowing Power on $500,000 Home |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Age 62** | ~36% | $180,000 |
| **Age 67** | ~41% | $205,000 |
| **Age 72** | ~46% | $230,000 |
| **Age 77** | ~52% | $260,000 |
| **Age 82** | ~59% | $295,000 |
| **Age 87+** | ~66%–72% | $330,000–$360,000 |

*Note: From this gross limit, upfront closing costs, lender origination fees, and the FHA upfront mortgage insurance premium (2% of MCA) are deducted.*

#### Step 2: How the Reverse Mortgage Loan Balance Grows
Because you make no monthly payments, the interest and mortgage insurance charges compound onto the principal balance:

$$B_t = B_{t-1} \times (1 + r_{\text{total}}) + \text{Monthly Draw}$$

Where the total monthly growth rate $r_{\text{total}}$ includes:
* The loan's interest rate (e.g., 6.5% annually)
* The annual FHA Mortgage Insurance Premium (0.5% annually)
* Monthly loan servicing fees (typically $25 to $35)

As time passes, your loan balance accelerates upward while remaining equity contracts.

## 4. Payout Options: How You Can Receive the Money

A reverse mortgage gives you multiple ways to access your funds:

1. **Lump Sum:** Receive a single payout at closing. (Federal rules typically cap the first-year draw at 60% of the principal limit unless paying off an existing forward mortgage).
2. **Tenure Payout (Monthly Income for Life):** Equal monthly cash payments for as long as at least one borrower lives in the home as their primary residence.
3. **Term Payout:** Equal monthly payments for a fixed period (e.g., 10 or 15 years).
4. **Line of Credit:** Access cash only when needed. Unused credit line balances grow automatically over time at the same compounding rate as the loan's interest, providing an expanding inflation hedge.
5. **Modified Combination:** A blend of a monthly tenure payment alongside a standby line of credit.

## 5. Pros and Cons of a Reverse Mortgage

### Advantages
* **Eliminates Existing Mortgage Payments:** If you still carry a forward mortgage balance in retirement, a reverse mortgage pays it off, freeing up hundreds or thousands in monthly cash flow.
* **Tax-Free Cash:** Reverse mortgage proceeds are loan advances, not earned income, meaning they do not increase your taxable income or affect Social Security or Medicare premiums.
* **Stay in Your Home:** You retain legal ownership and can remain in your home indefinitely, provided you maintain the property and pay taxes.
* **Non-Recourse Protection:** You or your heirs will never have to pay out-of-pocket if the home sells for less than the loan balance upon death.

### Disadvantages and Risks
* **High Upfront Closing Costs:** FHA upfront mortgage insurance (2% of home value), origination fees (up to $6,000), appraisal, and title costs can total $12,000 to $20,000+.
* **Erodes Inheritance:** As the loan balance compounds over 10 to 20 years, remaining home equity for your children or heirs shrinks dramatically.
* **Foreclosure Risk (The Big Trap):** You are still 100% legally required to pay property taxes, hazard insurance, and maintenance. If you fall behind on taxes or insurance, the lender can foreclose.
* **Impact on Needs-Based Benefits:** While Social Security and Medicare are unaffected, liquid funds sitting in your bank account could disqualify you from Medicaid or SSI.

---

## 6. What Happens to the Home After You Pass Away?

When the last surviving borrower passes away or moves out for 12 consecutive months, the reverse mortgage becomes due. Heirs typically have **6 months (with up to two 90-day extensions)** to choose one of three paths:

1. **Sell the Home:** The heirs sell the property on the open market, pay off the reverse mortgage balance from the sale proceeds, and pocket any remaining equity.
2. **Keep the Home:** The heirs can refinance or pay off the reverse mortgage for the lesser of the outstanding loan balance or **95% of the current appraised market value**.
3. **Walk Away (Deed in Lieu):** If the home is underwater (the loan balance exceeds the home's value), the heirs can simply hand the keys to the lender with zero liability or debt collections against the estate.

---

## 7. The Bottom Line: Who Should (and Shouldn't) Get One?

* **A reverse mortgage makes sense if:** You are 65+, plan to remain in your home for the rest of your life, need additional cash flow to cover health or living expenses, and have adequate funds to cover property taxes and home maintenance.
* **Avoid a reverse mortgage if:** You plan to move or downsize within the next 3 to 5 years, want to pass the home debt-free to heirs, or struggle to afford property taxes, utilities, and homeowners insurance.
