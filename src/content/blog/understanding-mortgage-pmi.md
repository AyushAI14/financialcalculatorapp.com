When buying a home, you might notice an extra fee on your monthly payment estimate labeled **PMI**, or **Private Mortgage Insurance**. 

For many buyers, this feels like a penalty. But understanding what PMI is and how it works is the first step to saving thousands of dollars over the life of your home loan.

---

## 1. What is PMI?
PMI is insurance that conventional lenders require if your down payment is **less than 20%** of the home's purchase price. 

Here is the most important thing to know: **PMI does not protect you.** It protects the *lender* in case you stop making your mortgage payments. 

Because you are putting down less than 20%, the lender views your loan as higher risk. PMI is their safety net, paid for by you.

---

## 2. How much does PMI cost?
PMI typically costs between **0.5% and 1.5%** of your total loan amount annually. 

For example, on a $400,000 home loan, a 1% annual PMI fee is $4,000 per year—which adds an extra **$333 per month** to your mortgage payment!

The exact rate depends on:
* **Your Credit Score:** Better credit scores get lower PMI rates.
* **Your Down Payment:** Putting down 15% gets you a lower rate than putting down 3%.
* **Your Loan Term:** 15-year mortgages often have lower PMI than 30-year mortgages.

---

## 3. How do I get rid of PMI?
Unlike FHA loans (where mortgage insurance usually lasts forever), conventional PMI is temporary. You can get rid of it in two main ways:

### A. The Automatic Way (78% LTV)
By law, your lender must automatically cancel PMI once your loan balance drops to **78%** of the original value of your home through regular monthly principal payments.

### B. The Request Way (80% LTV)
You can write to your lender and request that they remove PMI once your balance reaches **80%** of the original home value. Don't wait for them to do it automatically—call them the moment you hit 20% equity!

### C. Refinancing or Re-appraising
If home values in your neighborhood have risen significantly, your equity may now be over 20%, even if you haven't paid off much principal. You can pay for a new appraisal to prove to your lender that your loan-to-value ratio is below 80%.
