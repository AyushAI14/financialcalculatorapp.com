When shopping for a loan—whether a mortgage, auto loan, or personal loan—you will often see two rates advertised: the **Nominal Interest Rate** (or base rate) and the **Annual Percentage Rate (APR)**.

Understanding the difference between these two rates is crucial to avoiding expensive lending traps.

## What is the Nominal Interest Rate?
The nominal interest rate is the base cost of borrowing the principal amount. It is the percentage used by the lender to calculate the monthly interest charges accrued on your outstanding balance. For example, on a $300,000 mortgage with a nominal rate of 6%, your interest accrued in the first month is calculated directly using that 6% annual rate.

However, the nominal rate **does not include any other borrowing costs**.

## What is APR?
The Annual Percentage Rate (APR) represents the **total annual cost of borrowing**, expressed as a percentage. It incorporates both the nominal interest rate and any upfront fees, points, and other closing charges required to secure the loan.

These fees include:
* Loan origination fees
* Discount points (prepaid interest)
* Underwriting fees
* Private mortgage insurance (PMI)
* Broker commissions

Because these fees are factored into the APR, **the APR is almost always higher than the nominal rate**.

## How APR is Calculated
To calculate APR, the lender determines the standard monthly payment based on the nominal interest rate and the full loan principal.

However, the borrower does not actually receive the full principal amount. Instead, they receive the principal **minus the closing fees**. By setting the monthly payment against this lower "net proceeds" amount, we solve for a new, higher effective interest rate. That rate is the APR.
